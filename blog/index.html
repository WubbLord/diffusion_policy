Joint Deltas Are Not Joint Actions

Diffusion Policy can learn joint-space desired motion from proprioception, but deploying those labels through a different controller exposes an action-interface mismatch. A small inverse-controller adapter can recover strong performance on simple manipulation tasks, while contact-heavy tasks reveal the limits of local joint tracking.

Brian Zhang and Sourish Pramanik · Spring 2026

Introduction

Diffusion Policy [1] represents a robot policy as a conditional denoising process over future actions. Its empirical appeal is that it can predict high-dimensional action sequences, model multimodal demonstrations, and execute them in a receding-horizon loop. The original paper evaluates this idea mostly through the action interfaces exposed by each benchmark. In RoboMimic [2], those actions are operational-space end-effector commands for a robosuite [3] controller, not raw joint commands.

This project began with a simple question: if Diffusion Policy [1] is good at modeling high-dimensional trajectories, can we train the same architecture to output joint-space actions? The tempting label is available in every low-dimensional RoboMimic [2] trajectory: dq_demo[t] = q[t+1] - q[t]. That label is a real measured transition of the robot arm. However, it is not necessarily the command that would produce that transition under a joint-position controller.

Main claim: demonstration-derived joint deltas are useful desired transitions, but they are not executable actions unless they are mapped into the controller's command interface.

The experiments support this claim. On Can MH, the original end-effector Diffusion Policy [1] reaches 49/50 test successes. A joint-delta Diffusion Policy [1] executed directly through robosuite [3] JOINT_POSITION gets 0/50. The same joint-delta policy, passed through a learned inverse-controller adapter, reaches 50/50. The failure is therefore not simply that a diffusion model cannot predict joint-space motion. The failure is that the learned quantity and the deployment action interface are different.

Related Work

Diffusion Policy by Chi et al. [1] formulates visuomotor control as denoising over robot action sequences and reports strong performance across simulated and real manipulation benchmarks, including RoboMimic [2]. Two details are especially relevant here: it uses receding-horizon execution, and it finds a strong synergy between diffusion policies and position control. Our project keeps the same low-dimensional diffusion policy pipeline, but asks a lower-level question: when a position-like label is derived from the next observed joint state, is that label actually a command? The Can MH result says no. The policy can predict useful desired motion, but the deployment controller still needs the right command units.

RoboMimic by Mandlekar et al. [2] provides the demonstration datasets and benchmark tasks used here. It was designed to study imitation learning from offline human demonstrations, including PH proficient-human and MH multi-human datasets. robosuite [3] supplies the simulator and controller abstractions. That separation is central to this project: the dataset logs state transitions under one action interface, while our joint-space policy is evaluated under another.

Recent joint-space diffusion work attacks the harder problem of producing kinematically valid robot motion. HDP introduces RK-Diffuser [4], which generates both end-effector and joint-position trajectories and uses differentiable kinematics to distill end-effector accuracy into the joint trajectory. KStar Diffuser [5] adds a URDF-derived spatial-temporal graph and a differentiable-kinematics loss for bimanual manipulation. KADP [6] instead represents states and actions as 3D points on the robot body, then recovers joint commands with whole-body inverse kinematics. RobotDiffuse [7] studies diffusion for redundant-manipulator motion planning with obstacle constraints. These methods improve the policy or action representation itself. Our contribution is narrower but complementary: with the standard Diffusion Policy [1] backbone and RoboMimic [2] low-dimensional state, we isolate the action-interface mismatch and show that a small adapter can recover performance without changing the policy architecture.

The closest conceptual connection is state-only imitation learning. Radosavovic et al. [8] train an inverse dynamics model to infer actions from demonstrations that contain states but not expert actions. Liu et al. [9] similarly decouple state planning from inverse dynamics in DePO [9]. Our setting is more specific: RoboMimic [2] does have actions, but they are end-effector OSC commands. For a joint-position controller, the needed action is missing, so we learn an inverse-controller adapter from synthetic one-step probes. This turns a measured transition q[t+1] - q[t] into a command in the deployment controller's action space.

This work is also related to policy representations such as Implicit Behavioral Cloning [10] and Behavior Transformer [11], which address multimodal continuous actions. Our contribution is orthogonal: before comparing policy classes, the label must be a command that the evaluation controller can execute.

Methods

We modified the RoboMimic [2] low-dimensional Diffusion Policy [1] pipeline as little as possible. The joint-delta policy uses the same UNet diffusion workspace, horizon 16, observation history 2, action execution horizon 8, and 100 DDPM inference steps. The observation includes object state, end-effector pose, gripper proprioception, and joint position. The action is the arm joint transition plus the original gripper command.

The key addition is an adapter f(state, dq_desired) -> u, where u is a physical joint-position command. The adapter is a three-layer MLP with 512 hidden units per layer, SiLU activations, and layer normalization. Its state input is the full low-dimensional simulator state used for adapter training: object, end-effector position and quaternion, gripper qpos, joint position, and joint velocity.

Figure 1. The observed joint transition is treated as a desired motion. The adapter learns to convert that desired motion into a command for the deployment controller.

To train the adapter, we generated synthetic inverse-controller data by probing the simulator around demonstration states. For each RoboMimic [2] timestep, we reset robosuite [3] to the logged simulator state, read the full low-dimensional state s_t, and compute the observed demo transition dq_demo = q[t+1] - q[t]. We then sample 32 candidate physical joint-position commands u in radians, clip them to [-0.25, 0.25] per joint, convert them to the normalized controller range by dividing by 0.25, append the logged gripper command, and execute one JOINT_POSITION step.

The command samples are deliberately concentrated near plausible demo motion while still covering off-demo directions. The first anchors are 0, dq_demo, 2dq_demo, 4dq_demo, 8dq_demo, 16dq_demo, and -dq_demo. The remaining samples mix uniform commands over the full range, scaled-demo commands with factors in [0, 20] plus noise, and zero-mean Gaussian commands. After each probe, we measure the actual resulting transition dq_actual. Each training example is therefore (s_t, dq_actual) -> u: the adapter learns which command would have produced a desired local joint transition from that state. For Can MH alone this produced 2,008,192 one-step trials over 62,756 demonstration timesteps. Across tasks, PH adapters use demos 0:150 for training and 150:200 for validation, while MH adapters use 0:250 and 250:300.

Experiments

1. Is naive joint-space execution the problem?

The first diagnostic compares three Can MH policies under the same 50 seeded rollout protocol. The original end-effector policy uses the native OSC action interface. The naive joint-delta policy sends predicted joint deltas directly to JOINT_POSITION. The adapter policy uses the same joint-delta Diffusion Policy [1] but inserts the learned inverse controller before execution.

Figure 2. Direct joint-delta execution fails, while the adapter recovers Can performance. The original EEF baseline is included as the clearest available baseline.

The joint-delta policy was not obviously random. In an offline validation check conditioned on ground-truth teleoperated state, its arm joint-delta MAE was 0.006985 rad over 1,308 validation timesteps. The mean absolute target delta was 0.009394 rad. This is not perfect, but it is good enough that a 0/50 rollout result points to the interface mismatch rather than simply an untrained model.

2. Can the adapter replay held-out demonstrations?

Before using a learned policy, we tested the adapter with oracle desired deltas from held-out demonstrations. At each step, the desired motion is computed from the demo, but the adapter state is the live simulator state. This tests whether f can keep the robot near the intended joint trajectory after its own small errors accumulate.

The important lesson is that oracle replay success is not a direct score for adapter quality. The adapter's direct job is local: given the current simulator state and a desired joint transition, produce a command whose next-state delta matches that transition. The replay success column asks a harder question: if we chain those local translations through an entire task, do contacts and object state remain close enough for the original demonstration timing to still work?

Tool Hang and Transport expose why that distinction matters. Tool Hang is a long-horizon, contact-rich precision task: the robot has to grasp the tool with the right orientation, approach the frame, catch the hook, and release without knocking the object out of the intended contact mode. A small joint tracking error can leave the tool visually close but mechanically wrong. Transport is harder for a different reason: it is bimanual, has a 16-dimensional joint command, and requires coordinated object transfer between two arms. Transport PH has a modest replay delta MAE but only 17/50 replay success, which suggests that task-level coordination and contact timing are failing even when local joint tracking is not disastrous. Transport MH fails both locally and globally, with much larger delta error and 0/50 success.

3. Does the full joint-delta policy work beyond Can?

We trained joint-delta Diffusion Policies [1] for the available RoboMimic [2] low-dimensional variants, usually for 5000 epochs. The full pipeline combines the learned policy with the held-out-demo adapter. For context, Figure 4 also includes the original Diffusion Policy [1] paper's reported RoboMimic [2] state-policy results for the closest matching architecture, DiffusionPolicy-C.

Figure 4. The full joint-delta DP plus adapter pipeline reaches near-perfect success on Can and Lift, partial success on Square, fails on Tool Hang and Transport PH, and reaches 5/50 on Transport MH. The adjacent paper baseline is the original Diffusion Policy [1] state-policy DiffusionPolicy-C max success rate for the same task variant.

This result gives a more nuanced answer than "joint space works" or "joint space fails." On Can and Lift, desired joint transitions plus an adapter are enough. On Square, they are useful but not at the original Diffusion Policy [1] level. On Tool Hang and Transport, even the adapter and two-arm-compatible runner are not enough for reliable task completion.

4. Should the adapter be run as a closed-loop servo?

The final experiment asks whether a desired joint transition should be executed in one shot or iteratively. In closed-loop mode, the runner sets q_target = q_current + dq_desired. For k inner steps, it recomputes the residual q_target - q_current, sends that residual through the adapter, and steps the simulator. Thus, the first inner step uses the full desired delta and later steps try to remove the remaining error.

Figure 5. Each point is test success over 50 rollouts. Color identifies the task; dotted lines are PH datasets and solid lines are MH datasets. Gaps mark values that were still pending or timed out at the time of writing.

The closed-loop result is surprising at first: more servo iterations do not generally improve task success. Can MH drops from 50/50 at k=1 to 40/50 at k=2 and 12/50 at k=3. Lift MH is the main exception, with 50/50 at k=2. The reason is that k is not just extra adapter computation. Each inner step is a real robosuite [3] control step, so closed-loop execution changes the amount of simulated time spent on each policy action. Better local joint tracking can damage contact timing.

Discussion

Our central contribution is to make the action-interface mismatch explicit. RoboMimic [2] demonstrations contain both state transitions and controller actions, but those objects are not interchangeable. A measured joint transition tells us where the arm went; it does not tell us which command a different controller should receive to reproduce that motion. The Can MH diagnostic isolates this point sharply: the original end-effector policy succeeds, the naive joint-delta policy gets 0/50, and the same joint-delta policy recovers to 50/50 once its desired motion is translated through an inverse-controller adapter.

The second contribution is the adapter formulation itself. We treat dq = q[t+1] - q[t] as a desired local transition, not as an action. The learned adapter maps (state, dq_desired) into the command space of the deployment controller. This lets us keep the trajectory-modeling strengths of Diffusion Policy [1] while adding the missing controller semantics as a separate, testable module. The adapter is small, but it encodes an important prior: actions should be learned in the units and normalization used by the controller that will execute them.

The third contribution is diagnostic. We evaluate the adapter in oracle replay before using a learned policy, which separates policy error from controller-translation error. When oracle replay succeeds but full rollout fails, the policy is the likely bottleneck. When oracle replay fails, the desired transition is not being converted into a robust command sequence. This distinction matters because one-step delta MAE alone can look good while contact-rich rollouts still drift off the demonstration manifold.

The broader empirical contribution is a map of where this idea works. On Can and Lift, the adapter recovers high success and shows that joint-space desired motion is a viable representation for Diffusion Policy [1]. On Square, Tool Hang, and Transport, success drops when contact precision or multi-arm coordination dominates the task. The closed-loop sweep adds another negative result: repeatedly servoing the same desired delta does not reliably help, because each inner step also changes the timing of contact. Better local tracking is not automatically better task execution.

These results also define the limits of the current system. All experiments are in simulation, and the adapter assumes accurate low-dimensional state. Synthetic inverse-controller data is expensive: Can MH alone used over two million one-step probes. Command saturation is common, so some requested deltas exceed what the controller can realize in one step. Finally, the adapter is local. It can translate a desired joint transition, but it does not reason about object pose, future contacts, or two-arm coordination beyond what is already present in the policy output.

Conclusion

The main answer to the original question is yes, but with a condition. Diffusion Policy [1] can learn useful joint-space desired transitions, and on Can and Lift those transitions can drive high-success policies. However, observed joint deltas are not controller commands. The action interface must be modeled explicitly.

We think the broader lesson is that action representation is not only a question of dimensionality or policy architecture. It is also a question of semantics. A state transition, an end-effector command, a normalized joint-position command, and a torque are different objects, even when they all move the same robot. For imitation learning systems that reuse offline demonstrations under new controllers, the adapter between these objects can be as important as the policy itself.
